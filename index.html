03 / What happens during decompression?

1. Small inputThe application receives an archive that looks inexpensive to store or upload.
2. ExpansionDecompression reconstructs the original data. CPU time and memory or disk use can grow substantially.
3. Resource exhaustionIf the expanded output exceeds available resources, a service may slow down, fail, or become unavailable.

05 / Check your understanding

Why can a tiny compressed file still be dangerous?

Key concepts

How is this related to algorithmic complexity?

Compression ratio describes the relationship between encoded and decoded size; it is not itself a time-complexity class. Decompression work generally depends on the amount and structure of encoded data and the algorithm. An attacker exploits the gap between the cheap-to-handle input and the potentially expensive processing or output. Runtime can also be affected by pathological formats or implementation weaknesses.

Is every high compression ratio a zip bomb?

No. Legitimate data can compress very well. A high ratio is a risk signal, not proof of malicious intent. Defenses should combine explicit resource limits with sensible validation rather than relying on one ratio threshold alone.

Why is this a denial-of-service example?

Denial of service (DoS) occurs when a system's resources are exhausted or its service becomes unavailable. Decompression bombs are a resource-exhaustion example; the demonstration does not perform an attack.

Educational simulation only. No ZIP files are generated, uploaded, or decompressed. Use resource limits and safe failure handling in real systems.
